# Cognitive-Epistemic Performance: A New Genre of AI-Infused Art

The emerging art genre we describe here brings together **cognitive arts** and **knowledge arts** with creative technology. It integrates artificial intelligence (AI) and creative code with live performance, dynamic storytelling, and choreographed scores, drawing on the legacies of conceptual art, postdramatic theater, and relational aesthetics. In these works, narrative and choreography become _knowledge systems_: the performance itself explores questions of knowing, memory, and meaning. This synthesis reflects recent theoretical trends: cognitive science and epistemology inform the work’s mode of creation, while performance studies highlight embodied and participatory experience. In practice, artists use AI (neural networks, machine learning, generative algorithms) both to generate art-objects and to invite new forms of spectatorship. The genre thereby challenges traditional art categories by foregrounding **knowledge production** and **interaction**: art becomes a site of inquiry, discovery, and social encounter. This essay defines and contextualizes this genre, tracing its roots and highlighting how storytelling, choreography, and AI collaborate to create rich, participatory experiences.

---

## Theoretical Foundations: Cognition, Epistemology, and Performance

This genre rests on cognitive-scientific and epistemological premises. Cognitive science increasingly views the mind as embodied and distributed; knowledge is not only in the brain but “distributed across the body and environment.” In practice this means choreographic scores and improvisation are seen as embodying tacit knowledge and insight. For example, performers may access internal states (posture, breath, proprioception) to convey understanding that eludes explicit language. Research in embodied cognition thus encourages art that integrates mind, body, and context.

At the same time, the epistemic dimension of AI is foregrounded: recent work argues that modern AI (machine learning, neural networks, large language models) is fundamentally an epistemic technology, designed for knowledge tasks. AI systems parse data, detect patterns, and make predictions in order to generate new content. As philosopher Ramón Alvarado puts it, AI is developed and deployed to manipulate epistemic content (data) and to operate via epistemic processes like analysis and prediction. Within our genre, AI becomes a collaborator in constructing knowledge: for instance, generative AI models are trained on vast cultural datasets (e.g., art collections) and then used to produce narratives or imagery that reflect and reconfigure that knowledge. Artists treat AI as a kind of synthetic cognition—a “technology similar in complexity to the human brain”—whose operations are mysterious yet rich with potential for new forms of expression.

Performance studies also shape the field by redefining art as _process_ and _encounter_. Hans-Thies Lehmann’s notion of postdramatic theatre (1999) is instructive: postdramatic performance breaks with scripted narrative and mimesis, blending life and art and “challeng[ing] the traditional concept of mimesis.” In postdramatic works, story and meaning are not fixed texts but emerge _in situ_, often with audience participation or with technology mediating the event. Similarly, Nina Tecklenburg argues that contemporary narrative is no longer a prewritten script but an embodied cultural practice—a “participatory and open process” that creates and dissolves social bonds. Storytelling in this genre is thus dynamic and relational, an activity that performers and viewers co-create in real time. In summary, theoretical roots in cognitive science and epistemology encourage emphasis on _knowing-as-doing_: storytelling, movement, and code become ways of thinking that reveal something about cognition itself.

## Historical Antecedents: Conceptual Art, Postdramatic Theatre, Relational Aesthetics

Historically, this genre draws on three main lineages:

1. **Conceptual Art** (1960s–70s) insisted that ideas—rather than aesthetic form—are the core of art. Conceptual art practices made artworks into thought experiments: Joseph Kosuth’s slogan “Art as Idea as Idea” exemplifies this mindset. Philosophers of conceptual art note that it foregrounds cognitive value; “knowledge and cognitive value” are “at the very heart” of the conceptual project. Conceptual artists asked viewers to engage intellectually and critically, often substituting written or performed instructions for physical objects. Our emerging genre inherits this emphasis on idea over object, treating performance and code as vehicles for inquiry. For instance, a coded choreography might be accompanied by a written formula, blurring the line between a dance score and a logical proof. By continuing the lineage of conceptual art, cognitive-epistemic performances justify themselves as investigations: they produce understanding or questions rather than souvenirs or artifacts.

2. **Postdramatic Theatre** (Lehmann 1999; Carlson 2015) provides a template for mixing narrative fragments, multimedia, and audience involvement. Lehmann coined the term _postdramatic_ to describe theatre that abandons linear plot in favor of “intermedial and non-narrative” modes. Marvin Carlson notes that contemporary directors like Ivo van Hove or Punchdrunk often reject traditional fiction, blending everyday life and performance and dissolving the boundary between stage and world. In these performances, the “theatre world” is no longer a self-contained fiction but an interstice in everyday life. Our new genre uses similar strategies: stories might be fed into an AI that responds to the audience, or choreographic sequences might be modulated by algorithmic parameters, making the line between scripted and spontaneous blurry. Postdramatic theatre also tends to privilege the _event_ over the _text_, much as generative art privileges code over static object. Thus, the emergent genre aligns with postdramatic ideas: narrative and character are tools for investigation, not ends in themselves.

3. **Relational Aesthetics** (Nicolas Bourriaud, 1998) inspires the social dimension of these works. Bourriaud defined relational art as art whose medium is human interaction and its social context. Instead of an autonomous symbolic object, relational art produces a “specific sociability”—a shared space of encounter. Bourriaud describes its themes as “being-together” and “the encounter between viewer and [work],” with “collective elaboration of meaning.” Many cognitive-epistemic performances are explicitly participatory: spectators might contribute story fragments, make choreographic choices, or influence AI-generated outcomes. In this way, the artwork takes place partly in the relations it engenders. The social network of ideas and people becomes visible: each person’s input shapes the ongoing narrative or score. Relational aesthetics thus provides the vocabulary for understanding the participatory mode of these works. In sum, the new genre synthesizes conceptual art’s focus on ideas, postdramatic theatre’s fluid form, and relational art’s interactive ethos.

## Storytelling and Choreography as Dynamic Knowledge Systems

In this genre, storytelling and choreography function as _living knowledge systems_. Unlike fixed scripts or recorded performances, these elements are treated as processes that generate understanding.

- **Collective Improvisation**  
  Storytelling may unfold as collective improvisation, augmented by digital elements. As Nina Tecklenburg notes, narration becomes “sensate, embodied cultural practice”—an activity where stories are felt, enacted, and exchanged in the moment. For example, a piece might invite the audience to contribute lines to an AI-driven narrative generator, so the story literally writes itself through collaboration. Each fragment of dialogue or gesture carries knowledge—personal memory, cultural myth, or conceptual pattern—which the group assembles. In this sense, narrative knowledge is emergent and distributed: the performers do not simply recite a text, they _collectively think through_ it.

- **Algorithmic Scores**  
  Choreographic scores are conceived as dynamic knowledge. Dance is often thought of as movement for its own sake, but here choreography encodes information: the sequencing of bodies becomes a kind of text or algorithm. These works often employ body-based rules (e.g., move according to data, or follow an AI’s prompt). According to Rankanen’s research, dance improvisation can reveal “information about a person’s experience of their internal space” (posture, breath, heartbeat). By attending to how environments and exercises affect movement, choreographers make tacit knowledge explicit. In a performance, a dancer’s intuitive response to sound or code is a translation of embodied cognition into visible form. Importantly, audiences interpret the choreography by aligning it with their own schemas, creating new knowledge together. Here the score is not an inert map but a starting point for inquiry: performers and spectators alike learn through the live unfolding of movement.

Thus, storytelling and choreography in these works operate much like interactive simulations of knowing. They are subject to change (open codes, modular narratives) and to remixing (through AI or audience input), so that each performance generates novel connections. As Tecklenburg suggests, narrative in this mode is “a participatory and open process”—meaning that knowing comes into being through the act of performance itself.

## AI and Creative Code: Generation and Reception

Artificial intelligence and creative code are central both to making these works and to how they are experienced:

- **Generative AI**  
  Generative AI algorithms (text, image, and motion models) extend the artist’s toolset. For instance, a neural network can be trained on a corpus of stories, choreography, or scientific data and then used to propose variations in real time. Lev Manovich observes that modern neural nets, trained on “trillions” of words or billions of images, can output texts and visuals on par with skilled professionals. These outputs are synthesized via layers of network “neurons” whose complexity rivals the human brain. Artists leverage this complexity to produce art that surprises even them. For example, an AI storyteller might remap personal anecdotes into mythic forms, or an algorithmic dancer might recombine gesture primitives into new patterns. Because the AI’s workings are opaque (parameters distributed across billions of connections), its contributions resemble the intuitive leaps of human creativity, yet they are different in origin and scale.

- **Creative Coding**  
  On the code side, creative coding (using languages like Processing, Python, Max/MSP, etc.) allows artists to define rule-based behaviors for narrative and movement. A “choreographic score” might be literally coded as an algorithm: e.g., “for each motion sequence, apply noise and scale factors from sensor input.” Story logic can be implemented in software that responds to keywords or emotional cues. The generative nature of this code means each performance can be unique. In terms of reception, audiences may witness or even directly influence the code running the show. A spectator might speak a word, which an AI parser interprets to alter the next scene, or might move in the space, which a sensor network translates into sound or visual output. Thus, AI and code are woven into the artwork’s structure and also into the feedback loop with participants. In this way, technology is not a mere tool but an _epistemic partner_: it both generates aesthetic content and frames the context in which knowledge emerges.

## Participatory and Relational Experience

Finally, such works embody a _participatory, relational_ orientation. Building on Bourriaud’s relational aesthetics, the value of these works often lies in the encounters they produce. The boundary between artist and audience blurs: the people in the room co-create the event.

- **Shared Authorship**  
  In an AI-mediated storytelling performance, viewers’ inputs (spoken or gestural) become nodes in the narrative network. The resulting story is co-authored, and meaning arises socially.

- **Embodied Exchange**  
  Choreographic installations might invite visitors to join dancers, learning moves on the fly; the knowledge transmitted is relational—held between participants—rather than deposited in a museum. The art space becomes a temporary community or “domain of exchange” where collective creativity unfolds.

This relational approach also extends to how knowledge is shared. Since cognitive and AI processes can be opaque, artists often facilitate dialogue or reflection. After a performance, participants might discuss the AI’s behavior, linking it to human biases or abstract ideas. The art thus fosters social understanding: viewers become aware of the “knowledge” embedded in algorithms and bodies alike. In short, these works exemplify art as an _epistemic public space_—a site where people learn with and from each other, as much as from the work itself.

---

## Conclusion

In summary, this novel art genre weaves together ideas from cognitive science (embodied knowledge), epistemology (art as inquiry), and performance (embodied enactment). It inherits from conceptual art the primacy of idea and questions; from postdramatic theatre the collapse of fiction and life; and from relational aesthetics the emphasis on social encounter. Storytelling and choreography are reconceived as dynamic knowledge practices, and AI/creative code serve as both medium and catalyst. These works offer experiences that are at once intellectual, sensorial, and communal. By doing so, they challenge spectators to become active knowers—co-explorers of meaning—in a space where technology and humanity meet. As Donna Haraway insightfully reminds us, in our “mythic” present we are “all chimeras, theorized and fabricated hybrids of machine and organism.” The art of this genre lives that hybridity: it is artmaking as ongoing dialogue among minds, machines, and bodies, perpetually reinventing how we conceive knowledge and experience.
