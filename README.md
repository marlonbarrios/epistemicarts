# Cognitive-Epistemic Performance and Born in Latent Space

The term **cognitive-epistemic performance** denotes an emerging hybrid art form that merges live performance with computational cognition and knowledge production. In this genre, a performer collaborates in real time with AI systems (GANs, LLMs, etc.), using creative coding, storytelling, movement, and sound to stage dynamic processes of meaning-making. Marlon Barrios Solano’s lecture-performance *Born in Latent Space* epitomizes this approach. As Solano describes it, the piece unfolds as “a speculative, real-time collaboration between the artist and machine learning models.” Seated at a laptop, the performer manipulates prompts and gestures so that the AI generates text, images, and audio in response. By this means the interface itself becomes “a stage for critical fictioning and embodied speculation.” In other words, knowledge is enacted and transformed live: the system constantly learns, adapts, and produces, blurring the line between human creativity and machine cognition. This practice draws on epistemological ideas (how we know, how knowledge is produced) as well as on cybernetic feedback loops, hence the term “cognitive-epistemic.” It is an explicitly epistemic form of performance art—one that generates and questions knowledge as it unfolds on stage.

The name *Born in Latent Space* highlights the key concept of **latent space** in machine learning. In AI, a latent space is an abstract, high-dimensional space where complex data is encoded into compressed features. As Solano notes, latent space “refers to a high-dimensional abstract space where data’s intrinsic, hidden features are represented in a compressed form.” Generative models (GANs, transformers, etc.) learn to navigate this space: by moving through latent dimensions they can produce new images or texts that resemble the training data yet are novel. James Bridle poetically calls latent space “the unseeable space in which machine learning makes its meaning”—“beyond that which we are incapable of visualizing” lie realms we have difficulty even understanding. In *Born in Latent Space*, this technical notion is reimagined as a poetic and political territory. Solano treats latent space as a realm of “all possible stories”—an unstable, generative dimension where memory, desire, identity, and imagination recombine through algorithmic play. Instead of just the math behind the scenes, latent space becomes a stage for experimenting with narrative and subjectivity: personal and collective memories flow through the network, generating hybrid images and voices that carry layered meanings.

## Conceptual Lineage: Conceptual Art, Postdramatic Theatre, and Relational Aesthetics

*Born in Latent Space* also extends several twentieth-century art and theatre currents. Its affinities with **conceptual art** are evident: here the idea and process are primary, and the material “object” of art (a theatrical spectacle) is subordinated to the intellectual concept. Solano explicitly references Bruce Nauman’s famous conceptual-performance *Walking in an Exaggerated Manner Around the Perimeter of a Square* (1967–68) as one vignette. In his own enactment, Solano “uses the square as a metaphor for inhabiting the alien—performing the alienation that stands in contrast to the normative, disciplined body.” He notes that by exaggerating movement he “interrogates the boundaries of conceptual experimentation, viewing it through the lens of white privilege.” In other words, Solano adopts Nauman’s minimalist gesture but shifts the conceptual frame: the piece critiques how the avant-garde’s conceptual rules have been canonized by elite (largely white) institutions and excludes other bodies and narratives. This echoes the conceptual-art tradition of Duchamp and Kosuth, where the idea is foregrounded over form (Joseph Kosuth wrote that “all art… is conceptual… because art only exists conceptually”). Similarly, Solano’s practice uses instructions, code, and text as artistic media, challenging the audience to contemplate the concept (in this case, the politics of AI and identity) as much as the spectacle.

The performance is also **postdramatic** in Lehmann’s sense. Hans-Thies Lehmann coined “postdramatic theater” to describe contemporary performance that goes beyond traditional text and narrative. In *Born in Latent Space*, narrative is fragmented into vignettes and the focus shifts to present bodies, data, and interfaces. Lehmann observes that postdramatic works give precedence to “breath, rhythm and the present actuality of the body’s visceral presence” over a coherent, scripted storyline. Indeed, Solano’s lecture-performance emphasizes gestures, dance scores, and live coding interactions rather than a linear plot. The voiceover stories (often delivered in first person) come in pieces, sometimes even re-performed or transformed by AI, suggesting multiple layers of meaning rather than a fixed narrative. This aligns with Lehmann’s notion of “concept theatre”: like Fluxus happenings and performance art of the 1970s, Solano’s work avoids mere representation and instead offers an “unmediated experience of the real (time, space, body).” The authority of a singular text is suspended; what remains is the “scenic composition” of voice, image, sound and movement itself, which provokes questions rather than delivering answers.

Another relevant context is **relational aesthetics** (Nicolas Bourriaud). Relational artists create situations or events that foreground human interactions and social contexts over autonomous objects. Bourriaud defined relational art as practices that take “the whole of human relations and their social context” as their point of departure. *Born in Latent Space* similarly foregrounds encounters—though some are between human and machine rather than person-to-person. The performance “invites audiences into the speculative and affective edges of AI—a space where presence is recombinant, identity is mutable, and data becomes poetic matter.” In this sense the audience is not a passive viewer of a static artwork but a participant in a live event where social and technical exchanges happen. The performer becomes a catalyst for new relations: between self and algorithm, between personal story and digital image, between history and simulated future. Solano even makes the technology partly open-source and interactive, encouraging the public to engage with the underlying models. This echoes Bourriaud’s view of the artist as facilitator rather than auteur.

More generally, scholars of digital theatre argue that contemporary performance has become an **“epistemological apparatus.”** That is, the stage is now explicitly a site of knowledge production and questioning. A recent special issue on hybrid performance speaks of changing “perceptual and epistemological conditions of digital theatre” and even reconceives theatre as a medium for knowing itself. *Born in Latent Space* certainly functions on this model: it is not simply aesthetic display but an exploration of “deep, unseen dimensions of data, cultural memory and language,” actively making the audience reflect on how AI systems shape what we know.

## Born in Latent Space: Structure, Methods, and Technologies

Structurally, *Born in Latent Space* is organized as a series of distinct **vignettes** or “nodes,” each roughly 3–7 minutes long, interweaving story, movement, and generative media. Between segments, Solano may code or prompt the AI, triggering new outputs that then feed into the next scene. The performer uses voice, gesture, and even eye-tracking or keyboard input to steer the system, which in turn projects images and text back to him (and the audience) in real time. The overall effect is like a lecture or conference presentation, but the content continually morphs based on the AI’s responses. Thus the piece enacts a live cognitive collaboration: at once performer-led and machine-led, human-guided and algorithmically emergent.

To realize this, Solano built custom software and leverages multiple AI tools. As he lists, *Born in Latent Space* employs platforms such as p5.js and Next.js for coding interactive interfaces, and it integrates several AI models:

- **Creative coding frameworks** (p5.js, Next.js) for designing the visual and interactive front-end  
- **Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)** for producing live images and video  
- **Large Language Models (LLMs)** (e.g. GPT-like systems) for generating text, dialogue, and even poetic responses  
- **Real-time body/gesture recognition** (using tools like MediaPipe) to translate the performer’s movements into data  
- **Other machine-learning models** for tasks like emotion or voice recognition  

These technologies allow the performer to, for example, walk in a pattern and have the system produce color shifts or images reflecting that movement, or to speak a story and receive a textual or visual improvisation back. This blend of tools foregrounds the work’s technical hybridity: it is equally code-driven and choreographed. The code and hardware become part of the actor’s “costume” and stage, and conversely the human body becomes an instrument for data generation.

Economy and contest are built into these methods. Solano often manipulates AI prompts on the fly, feeding in new words or sentences and then “responding to the outputs.” In this way he “reverses the hegemonic narratives of generative AI.” For example, if a pre-trained model produces a biased or stereotyped image, Solano might alter his input or interpret the result in a way that undermines that bias. This live prompt-coding is an epistemic act: he is literally steering the knowledge construction. In Cossette and Salter’s words, such work treats neural networks as potential performers themselves, asking whether “the mathematical entities called neural networks” can be “expressive and ‘perform’” alongside the human. Indeed, *Born in Latent Space* levels the field: both human and machine contribute actions, and both are subject to the others’ control loops.

Cossette and Salter further observe that today’s performances with AI create “deep epistemological and ontological questions” about how algorithms conceive of humans and society. Solano’s piece embodies that precisely: in generating images of people (e.g. the “Mr. Parker” faces below) and dialogues, it lays bare the machine’s “conception” of identity. By contrast, the human performer’s interiority—his feelings, memories, queerness—remains ineffable in a way machines can only approximate. The live juxtaposition of the two produces new knowledge: we learn about the biases in the machine’s latent space as well as about the performer’s own “cognitive recombinations” and how they inform alternate narratives.

## Themes and Theoretical Resonances

Several interlocking themes animate *Born in Latent Space*, as Solano himself enumerates: **queer longing and techno-intimacy**, **diasporic memory and migration**, and **synthetic cognition and speculation**. These are not tacked-on topics but emerge organically from the vignettes’ content and form.

A central thread is **queer techno-intimacy**. The performance repeatedly merges queer experience with machine agency. Solano’s narrator (a thinly veiled version of the artist) is often in states of longing, melancholy, or desire, and these are mapped onto algorithms. In one segment he recalls being an eight-year-old in Venezuela entranced by a fluently-Spanish-speaking American stranger, “Mr. Parker.” The young boy’s fascination—his goosebumps, his feeling of being spoken to at a distance—is tender and liminal. It evokes a queer awakening between two male bodies under the sun, tied together by language and unspoken affect. Haraway’s cyborg metaphor helps interpret this: the cyborg embraces “intimacy” beyond conventional boundaries. Haraway writes that the cyborg is “committed to… partiality, irony, intimacy, and perversity,” resisting fixed identity categories. Likewise, Solano’s persona is both human and machine-code; his gendered desire is mediated through AI imagery. The very act of generating digital portraits of Mr. Parker and overlaying them with a love-struck narrative creates a space of queer-technological eroticism. As *Born in Latent Space* itself declares, it stages “queer longing and techno-intimacy.” In this way the work participates in a long lineage of queer cyberfeminist thought, where sexuality and technology co-construct each other, and emotional subjectivity flows through circuits as well as bodies.

Another set of themes involves **diasporic memory and migration**. Solano, a Colombian-Venezuelan artist living in the U.S., weaves his personal and cultural history into the piece. The “Mr. Parker” monologue itself is set in Venezuela, with the narrator’s grandmother working for an American oil company boss. The foreign presence (a Texan in Venezuela) and the mixed-language exchange evoke colonial and migratory currents. More broadly, the performance nods to “migration, displacement, and diasporic memory.” For example, in one vignette a narrative of a grandmother traveling triggers an image-generation process, and elsewhere subsonic Morse-code gestures recall coded messages passed across borders. In Solano’s design, even the latent space metaphor connects to diaspora: he describes it as an “ethereal space… where desire, affect, memory, longing and hybrid materiality” combine. In these ways the piece suggests that migrants’ intangible memories (voices, dreams, biases) become encoded into the generative system, echoing cultural transmission. It enacts a kind of “algorithmic archive” of diasporic sensation.

Closely linked is the theme of **synthetic cognition and AI ethics**. The work explicitly probes how generative systems reflect and distort our worldviews. Vignettes titled “I am in Love with Alan Turing” or “Entropic Haiku” turn AI’s logical processes into poetic subjects. Solano often draws attention to the limitations of the AI’s “mind”: when a model misinterprets a prompt or hallucinates an image, he playfully anthropomorphizes it (talking to the machine as “you”). In doing so he raises questions about what it means for an AI to “know” something. The listed themes include “AI ethics, recursion, and synthetic cognition,” pointing to a critique of how black-box systems are trained on biased data. By tweaking prompts and feeding back outputs, Solano engages in a kind of speculative AI research onstage—a live thought experiment. This aligns with Lev Manovich’s observation that generative AI heralds “a true revolution in media creation”: Solano participates in that revolution by handing creative agency partially to the algorithm and inviting the audience to witness its biases and potentials.

Underlying all this is **critical speculation**. *Born in Latent Space* does not simply depict a deterministic future ruled by machines; it asks “what if?” What if personal history could be remixed by code? What if the “singularities” in latent space produce not utopias but haunted palimpsests of memory? Solano’s narrative is speculative fiction in action: the performance conjures alternative stories of identity and technology. By “manipulating prompts” Solano literally scripts counter-narratives that challenge the default “hegemonic narratives of generative AI.” Thus the piece serves as a form of cognitive activism—using performance art to imagine more inclusive or imaginative futures.

## Case Studies: Two Vignettes

Two particularly illustrative vignettes are **“Walking in an Exaggerated Manner Around the Perimeter of a Square”** and **“We Are All Looking for Mr. Parker.”** The former directly references conceptual art history (Nauman’s work) while the latter is a deeply personal story transformed by AI.

### Walking in an Exaggerated Manner Around the Perimeter of a Square
In this vignette the stage is dark except for a spotlight on Solano and a large screen behind him. On the screen plays Nauman’s original video (a figure pacing a green square). Solano, in turn, paces the same square in an over-emphasized way. Through this restaging, he interrogates Nauman’s assumptions. As the program notes explain, he uses “the square as a metaphor for inhabiting the alien—performing the alienation that stands in contrast to the normative, disciplined body.” In practice, Solano’s gait is deliberately awkward, and his body shape and skin color differ from Nauman’s. He thereby critiques how experimental art forms became “an exclusive realm of white privilege.” In doing so, he extends conceptual art: Nauman’s original was about language and self (the title repeats a sentence), whereas Solano’s is about power and identity. The AI component in this vignette might show morphing shapes or color shifts in response to his motion, making the “latent square” literally dynamic. Thus this nod to conceptualism becomes a layered commentary on embodiment, space, and the unseen “grid” of social structures (a literal latent space).

### We Are All Looking for Mr. Parker
The “Mr. Parker” vignette is more directly narrative and exemplifies the fusion of personal memory with AI generation. In this piece, Solano recites a childhood memory in which he felt a mysterious attraction to an older man on his grandmother’s farm in Venezuela. The text is poetic and fragmentary: “It was like hearing something far off… Goosebumps rose on my arms… something like seeing a shadow before it arrives… The words didn’t matter… It stayed with me.” As he speaks, the screen shows a grid of faces—each a computer-generated portrait of the blonde Mr. Parker, filtered through an AI. These faces are neither realistic photos nor abstract; they have the uncanny quality of an AI dreaming. Meanwhile, ambient queer-tinged music plays. The overall effect is one of reverie and longing. Here, we see the diasporic element (American Mr. Parker in Venezuela), the queer longing (the boy’s embodied reaction), and the techno-intimacy (the voiceover over AI-made images). By juxtaposing the narrator’s human voice with the machine’s fragmented visage of Mr. Parker, the vignette dramatizes the gap—and connection—between flesh and code.

In analytical terms, this vignette also illustrates “latent space” at work. The AI’s facial portraits derive from a latent space trained on many human faces; Solano’s prompts and the boy’s memory steer which aspects emerge (blond hair, sunburned skin, shy smile). The narrative even mentions “Mr. Parker’s eyes,” highlighting gaze and perception. Through the performance, Solano invites us to consider: *What does Mr. Parker look like through the machine’s eyes?* The human narrator’s intimate feeling fills in what the AI does not know. In this way the performance makes latent space concrete: it’s both the internal square of the Nauman piece and the amalgam of Mr. Parker’s AI-generated portrait gallery.

## Conclusion: Toward a New Epistemic Theatre

*Born in Latent Space* exemplifies how contemporary performance can be epistemic: it stages not just a spectacle, but a knowing process. By blending conceptual-art critique (as in the Nauman homage), postdramatic theatricality (decentered narrative, focus on presence), and the social engagement of relational art, Solano forges a new form. He uses every tool—GANs, LLMs, live coding, dance—to foreground how knowledge itself is made and unmade. Audiences witness an AI “thinking” and a human thinking in tandem. The work thus resonates with recent scholarship: scholars note that digital theatre is evolving the “epistemological conditions” of art, and *Born in Latent Space* takes this up as praxis.

Moreover, the piece engages theorists like Haraway, Lehmann, Bourriaud, Manovich, and Bridle without resorting to jargon. Haraway’s cyborg intimacy helps explain the performer’s human-machine entanglement. Lehmann’s postdramatic ideas underscore the focus on event over text. Bourriaud’s relational aesthetics appear in the collaborative, socially aware framing. Manovich’s observation that generative AI marks “a true revolution in media creation” is made manifest as algorithms become co-authors. Bridle’s notion of latent space as an “unseeable” realm is made narratively visible. Through *Born in Latent Space*, these concepts are not just cited but enacted.

In sum, cognitive-epistemic performance like Solano’s is about making knowledge tangible—and critically examining it. It transforms algorithms into co-performers and storytelling into speculative computing. As AI grows pervasive, such work becomes vital cultural critique: it shows us what it means to be “born” into a space shaped by unseen machines, and asks us who we are when our stories can be coded as data.
